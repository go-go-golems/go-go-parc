Yes — your note is basically pointing at the right distinction. The important difference is **what relation is used to connect a program $B$ to its result $R$**.

### 1. $eval^{=_{r}}_r$: the equational evaluator

This evaluator uses the **equivalence relation generated by the rules $r$**.

Roughly,

$$
eval^{=_{r}}_r(B)=R
\quad\text{when}\quad
B =_r R
$$

Here $=_r$ is built by taking the rules and allowing **reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity**.

So if a rule says

$$
B_1 \to_r B_2
$$

then equational reasoning lets you use it in either direction:

$$
B_1 =_r B_2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
B_2 =_r B_1.
$$

That's why it feels "non-directional." You're saying two expressions are **equal according to the language's equations**, not describing a sequence of computation steps.

---

### 2. $eval^{\twoheadrightarrow_r}_r$: the directional evaluator

This one uses the **reduction relation**:

$$
eval^{\twoheadrightarrow_r}_r(B)=R
\quad\text{when}\quad
B \twoheadrightarrow_r R
$$

where

$$
B \twoheadrightarrow_r R
$$

means something like:

$$
B \to_r B_1 \to_r B_2 \to_r \cdots \to_r R.
$$

This is the reflexive-transitive closure of reduction, plus the compatible/contextual machinery described in your notes.

Crucially, **there is no symmetry**. If

$$
B_1 \to_r B_2
$$

you cannot automatically conclude

$$
B_2 \to_r B_1.
$$

So this one really describes **computation going forward**.

$$

> Why is = larger than ->> what does it mean?

“Larger” means **it contains more related pairs of expressions**.

A relation is just a set of pairs. So if we have:

$$
A \to B \to C
$$

then the reflexive-transitive reduction relation $\twoheadrightarrow$ contains pairs like

$$
(A,A), (B,B), (C,C), (A,B), (B,C), (A,C).
$$

It says: “starting from the left expression, I can reduce forward to the right expression.”

But $=$ is an **equivalence relation**, so it also has symmetry. Therefore from

$$
A = B
$$

you also get

$$
B = A.
$$

So in this example, $=$ would additionally contain things like

$$
(B,A), (C,B), (C,A).
$$

In fact:

$$
\{(A,A),(A,B),(A,C),(B,B),(B,C),(C,C)\}
$$

is the sort of thing $\twoheadrightarrow$ gives, while $=$ can contain

$$
\{(A,A),(A,B),(A,C),(B,A),(B,B),(B,C),(C,A),(C,B),(C,C)\}.
$$

So we write informally:

$$
\twoheadrightarrow \subseteq =
$$

and say $=$ is “larger.”

The key reason is **symmetry**:

$$
A \twoheadrightarrow B
$$

does **not** imply

$$
B \twoheadrightarrow A,
$$

but

$$
A = B
$$

does imply

$$
B = A.
$$

So “larger” does not mean the $=$ evaluator is somehow better or computes more. It means the underlying relation identifies **more pairs of expressions** as related.

A good mental picture is:

$$
A \to B \to C
$$

for reduction: arrows only go forward.

For equality:

$$
A \leftrightarrow B \leftrightarrow C
$$

because once they are considered equivalent, you can reason in either direction.

That is exactly why the book later has to prove that even though $=$ is a larger relation than $\twoheadrightarrow$, they still give the **same final evaluation result**.

> And the reason we study ->> in the first place is that it’s actually easier to implement ?

Yes — that’s one of the main reasons.

The directional relation

$$
B \twoheadrightarrow_r R
$$

looks like an actual computation:

$$
B \to B_1 \to B_2 \to \cdots \to R.
$$

So an interpreter can essentially follow those arrows: find the next reducible expression, apply the appropriate rule, repeat until a result is reached.

By contrast, the equational relation

$$
B =_r R
$$

allows reasoning both forwards **and backwards** because of symmetry. That is useful mathematically, but it is not an obvious execution strategy. If you know

$$
B =_r B'
$$

you might be allowed to transform $B$ into $B'$ or $B'$ into $B$, and there may be many possible equations you could apply. That is much less like “run the program.”

So a useful distinction is:

$$
\boxed{\text{equational semantics: what expressions mean / are equivalent}}
$$

versus

$$
\boxed{\text{reduction semantics: how computation proceeds}}
$$

And that explains why the book wants to prove

$$
eval^{=_r}_r = eval^{\twoheadrightarrow_r}_r.
$$

If they give the same answers, then you get the best of both worlds: use $=_r$ when doing convenient mathematical reasoning, and use $\twoheadrightarrow_r$ when thinking about or implementing an evaluator.

So “easier to implement” is correct, but an even better phrase is: **$\twoheadrightarrow$ is closer to an executable description of the language.**

> What does consistency of the equational calculus mean?

In this chapter, **consistency of the equational calculus** means:

> The equations cannot make one program equal to two different final results.

Formally, if

$$
eval^{=_r}_r(B)=R_1
$$

and also

$$
eval^{=_r}_r(B)=R_2,
$$

then necessarily

$$
R_1=R_2.
$$

Suppose the only results are $t$ and $f$. An **inconsistent** equational calculus could somehow derive both

$$
B =_r t
$$

and

$$
B =_r f.
$$

Then, by symmetry and transitivity, you'd effectively get

$$
t =_r f,
$$

which is disastrous: the calculus can no longer distinguish the two answers.

Consistency guarantees that cannot happen.

This is exactly what **Theorem 2.1** on your page is saying:

$$
eval^{=_r}_r(B_0)=R_1
\quad\text{and}\quad
eval^{=_r}_r(B_0)=R_2
\implies
R_1=R_2.
$$

So the theorem establishes that $eval^{=_r}_r$ really is a **function**: one input cannot have two different outputs.

One subtle point: **consistency does not say every program has an answer.** It only says that if a program has an answer, it can't have two different ones.

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{consistency} = \text{at most one result}}
$$

whereas a later question is:

$$
\boxed{\text{termination/totality} = \text{does every program have a result?}}
$$

This is the same **syntax-vs-semantics pattern**, but Goldblatt is doing **model-theoretic semantics for logic**, whereas your other book is doing **semantics of programs by equations/reduction**.

On the Goldblatt page, you have a formal language $\mathcal L$: symbols such as $R,c,v_1,v_2,\ldots$, together with rules for building formulas. By themselves these are just syntax. Then Goldblatt gives them meaning by choosing a model

$$
\mathfrak A=\langle A,R,c\rangle.
$$

Here $A$ is the universe of things variables can range over, $R$ interprets the relation symbol $R$, and $c$ interprets the constant symbol $c$.

For formulas with free variables you also need a **valuation**

$$
x=(x_1,x_2,\ldots),
$$

which says

$$
v_1\mapsto x_1,\quad v_2\mapsto x_2,\ldots
$$

So the semantic question becomes something like

$$
\mathfrak A,x \models \varphi.
$$

That means:

> Under interpretation $\mathfrak A$, and with variables assigned according to $x$, the syntactic formula $\varphi$ is true.

This is very close in shape to evaluating a programming-language expression in an environment:

$$
\llbracket e\rrbracket_\rho = v,
$$

where $\rho$ tells you what the free variables mean.

So the correspondence is roughly:

| Goldblatt / logic | Programming languages |
|---|---|
| formula $\varphi$ | expression/program $B$ |
| model $\mathfrak A$ | interpretation of primitive operations |
| valuation $x$ | variable environment $\rho$ |
| sentence | closed program/expression |
| $\mathfrak A,x\models\varphi$ | evaluation gives some result |
| truth value $T/F$ | result $R$ |

The particularly important connection to your earlier discussion is that **equational calculus is on the syntactic side**, while Goldblatt's models are on the semantic side.

For example, suppose a calculus proves

$$
\varphi = \psi
$$

or, in logic,

$$
\vdash \varphi.
$$

That is a statement about what can be obtained by manipulating symbols according to formal rules.

By contrast,

$$
\mathfrak A\models \varphi
$$

is saying something about what the formula **means under an interpretation**.

That gives the classic distinction:

$$
\boxed{\text{syntax: what can be formally derived}}
$$

versus

$$
\boxed{\text{semantics: what is true in models}}
$$

And then mathematics asks whether the two agree. In logic this leads to **soundness and completeness**:

$$
\vdash\varphi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \models\varphi
$$

for soundness, and, for suitable logical systems,

$$
\models\varphi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vdash\varphi
$$

for completeness.

That is strongly analogous to what your programming-languages book was doing with

$$

$$

and

$$
eval^{\twoheadrightarrow_r}_r.
$$

There you have **two different formal ways of assigning behavior to the same program syntax**, and you prove that they give the same result.

There is also a deeper connection to **topos theory**. Goldblatt is building toward the idea that the ordinary set-theoretic semantics shown on this page can be generalized. In ordinary first-order semantics, variables range over elements of a set $A$, relations are subsets such as

$$
R\subseteq A\times A,
$$

and formulas are interpreted as truth conditions.

Categorically, these become:

$$
A \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad \text{an object}
$$

$$
R\subseteq A\times A
\quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
R\hookrightarrow A\times A
$$

where the relation is represented by a **subobject**.

And a formula with free variables

$$
\varphi(v_1,\ldots,v_n)
$$

can be interpreted as a subobject of the possible assignments:

$$
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket
\hookrightarrow
A^n.
$$

In **Set**, that subobject is literally the set

$$
\{(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\in A^n:
\varphi(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\text{ is true}\}.
$$

So Goldblatt's valuation $x$ is especially important: it's the elementary set-theoretic precursor of the categorical idea of plugging an assignment/generalized element into the interpretation of a formula.

For instance, if

$$
\varphi(v_1)=v_1Rc,
$$

then classically its interpretation is

$$
\llbracket \varphi\rrbracket

$$

A valuation saying $v_1\mapsto a$ makes $\varphi$ true precisely when

$$
a\in\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket.
$$

That's the bridge to topoi: **logical formulas become geometric objects—subobjects—rather than merely strings that receive $T/F$.**

$$
\boxed{
\text{formal syntax}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{some mathematical interpretation}
}
$$

and then you prove that the syntactic rules faithfully capture that interpretation. Goldblatt does this through **models and categorical semantics**; the reduction-semantics text does it through **equations and computation relations**.

L_1 =_r L_2,
$$

that proof is not automatically a **subderivation of the original proof $D$**. It may be newly constructed and could even be larger. The induction hypothesis only comes for free on the smaller trees already sitting underneath the current inference.

A useful picture is:

$$
\boxed{
r
\;\text{provides the primitive steps/equations}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
=_r
\;\text{is generated by inference rules}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{a derivation is a proof tree}
}
$$

So “induction on the derivation” literally means induction on the shape/height of that proof tree.


---

> How do I know that M =r L0 is “smaller”

Strictly speaking, **$M =_r L_0$ itself is not “smaller.”** The **derivation proving it** is smaller.

In the transitivity case, the derivation of

$$
M =_r N
$$

has the form

$$
\frac{
\boxed{\text{derivation }D_1\text{ of }M=_rL_0}
\qquad
\boxed{\text{derivation }D_2\text{ of }L_0=_rN}
}{
M=_rN
}
\text{ transitivity}.
$$

The whole proof tree is $D$. The proofs $D_1$ and $D_2$ are literally **subtrees** of $D$:

$$
D=
\begin{array}{c}
D_1\qquad D_2\\
\hline
M=_rN
\end{array}
$$

So if “size” means number of inference-rule applications,

$$
|D| = 1+|D_1|+|D_2|,
$$

and therefore

$$
|D_1|<|D|,
\qquad
|D_2|<|D|.
$$

Or, if you're inducting on height, their heights are also strictly less than the height of $D$.

### But how do you know the proof has that shape?

Because you're doing **induction on the derivation of $M=_rN$**.

You ask:

> What was the **last rule** used to produce $M=_rN$?

One possibility is transitivity. If the last rule was transitivity, then by the definition of the transitivity inference rule, the proof must have arisen from two earlier proofs:

$$
M=_rL_0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
L_0=_rN.
$$

Those earlier proofs are necessarily contained inside the current proof tree.

This is exactly analogous to structural induction on a syntax tree. If you have

$$
e = e_1 + e_2,
$$

then $e_1$ and $e_2$ are smaller because they are subexpressions used to construct $e$.

Here it's:

$$
\frac{M=_rL_0\qquad L_0=_rN}{M=_rN}.
$$

The two premises are the “children,” and the conclusion is the larger tree constructed from them.

So the safest wording is:

$$
\boxed{\text{The derivation of }M=_rL_0\text{ is smaller because it is a proper subderivation of the derivation of }M=_rN.}
$$

Not because $M$, $L_0$, or $N$ themselves are smaller expressions.

</details>

Yes — **if symmetry is the last rule used**, then the opposite equality is a smaller subderivation.

For example:

$$
\frac{
\boxed{D_0:\;N =_r M}
}{
M =_r N
}\;\text{sym}
$$

Here $D_0$, the derivation of $N=_rM$, is literally a subtree of the derivation of $M=_rN$. So the induction hypothesis applies to $D_0$.

The subtlety is that this is **not true for every derivation of $M=_rN$**. It depends on how that particular proof was constructed.

Suppose instead your current derivation is the transitivity one:

$$
\frac{
D_1:M=_rL_0
\qquad
D_2:L_0=_rN
}{
M=_rN
}\;\text{trans}
$$

Its subtrees are $D_1$ and $D_2$. There is no derivation of $N=_rM$ sitting inside this tree.

Of course, because symmetry is available, you can **construct** one:

$$
\frac{
\boxed{D:M=_rN}
}{
N=_rM
}\;\text{sym}
$$

But notice what happened: the original derivation $D$ is now **inside** the new derivation.

So

$$
\operatorname{size}(\text{proof of }N=_rM)
=
\operatorname{size}(D)+1.
$$

It's **larger**, not smaller.

That's the key distinction:

$$
\boxed{\text{“I can derive }N=_rM\text{”} \neq
\text{“the derivation of }N=_rM\text{ is a subderivation of my current proof.”}}
$$

When doing induction on a derivation, you only get the induction hypothesis automatically for the **premise derivations actually used in the final inference step**.

So when analyzing a derivation of $M=_rN$, you inspect its last rule:

- if last rule = **symmetry**, $N=_rM$ is the smaller premise;
- if last rule = **transitivity**, $M=_rL_0$ and $L_0=_rN$ are the smaller premises;
- etc.

This “specific proof tree, not merely the proposition being proved” point is the main conceptual trick in induction on derivations.

> So up to the church rosser lemma we can have a proof tree that derives from the definition of r, in fact two proof trees that arrive to the conclusions of the existence of L1 and L2 , but at that point we cannot apply induction because this is actually a new term in itself? We have two proof trees we proved using induction, but then we have to start “ fresh” to prove the existence of L3?

Yes. That is essentially the structure, with one terminology adjustment: the two things you get after applying the induction hypothesis are **reduction derivations**, not new equality proof trees.

Start with one equality derivation whose last rule is transitivity:

$$
\frac{
D_1:\ M=_r L_0
\qquad
D_2:\ L_0=_r N
}{
M=_r N
}
$$

Because $D_1,D_2$ are smaller subderivations, the induction hypothesis applies to each.

From $D_1$, IH gives some $L_1$ and reduction derivations

$$
M\twoheadrightarrow_r L_1
\qquad
L_0\twoheadrightarrow_r L_1.
$$

From $D_2$, IH gives some $L_2$ and reduction derivations

$$
L_0\twoheadrightarrow_r L_2
\qquad
N\twoheadrightarrow_r L_2.
$$

So now the situation is

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
M && L_0 && N\\
\downarrow && \swarrow\quad\searrow && \downarrow\\
L_1 &&&& L_2
\end{array}
$$

The induction hypothesis has done everything it is entitled to do.

Now you need to finish the original goal, which requires a **single** $L$ such that

$$
M\twoheadrightarrow L
\qquad
N\twoheadrightarrow L.
$$

You would like to fill in:

$$
L_1 \twoheadrightarrow L_3
\qquad
L_2 \twoheadrightarrow L_3.
$$

But there is no smaller equality subderivation available whose conclusion concerns $L_1$ and $L_2$. So induction on the original $=_r$ proof tree has nothing more to say.

That's the exact point where you need a **different theorem about the reduction relation itself**:

$$
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_1
\quad\land\quad
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_2
$$

implies

$$
\exists L_3.\;
L_1\twoheadrightarrow L_3
\land
L_2\twoheadrightarrow L_3.
$$

That's Church–Rosser/confluence.

Then you can concatenate paths:

$$
M\twoheadrightarrow L_1\twoheadrightarrow L_3
$$

so

$$
M\twoheadrightarrow L_3,
$$

and similarly

$$
N\twoheadrightarrow L_2\twoheadrightarrow L_3,
$$

so

$$
N\twoheadrightarrow L_3.
$$

And now the transitivity case is complete.

So your “start fresh” intuition is good. More precisely:

$$
\boxed{
\text{The induction on }=_r\text{ has reached the limit of what its IH gives us.}
}
$$

We then temporarily leave that proof and prove a **separate lemma about $\twoheadrightarrow_r$**. Once that lemma is established, we plug it back into the unfinished transitivity case.

The only part I'd change in your wording is “because $L_1,L_2$ are a new term.” The obstacle isn't that they're new *terms*. The obstacle is that **we do not possess smaller $=_r$-derivations involving $L_1$ and $L_2$** to which the induction hypothesis could apply.

For the result you’ve been discussing—**Lemma 2.2**

\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\exists L.\;M\twoheadrightarrow_r L\ \land\ N\twoheadrightarrow_r L,

there are actually **two different “trees” to keep separate**.

### 1. The input is a proof tree of $M=_rN$

For example, suppose the particular proof of $M=_rN$ ends with transitivity:

$$
\frac{
\boxed{D_1:\;M=_rL_0}
\qquad
\boxed{D_2:\;L_0=_rN}
}{
M=_rN
}
\;\text{trans}
$$

The boxes $D_1,D_2$ might themselves be huge proof trees. They are the smaller subderivations.

The induction says:

> Assume Lemma 2.2 already works for $D_1$ and $D_2$, and use that to prove it for the whole tree.

So applying the IH to $D_1$:

$$
\frac{D_1:M=_rL_0}
{
\exists L_1.\;
M\twoheadrightarrow L_1
\land
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_1
}
\text{ IH}
$$

and to $D_2$:

$$
\frac{D_2:L_0=_rN}
{
\exists L_2.\;
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_2
\land
N\twoheadrightarrow L_2
}
\text{ IH}.
$$

At this point you have

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
M && L_0 && N\\
\downarrow && \swarrow\quad\searrow && \downarrow\\
L_1 &&&& L_2
\end{array}
$$

and **the induction has given you everything it can**.

Now comes the extra lemma.

From

$$
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_1
\qquad
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_2
$$

Church–Rosser gives:

$$
\frac{
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_1
\qquad
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_2
}{
\exists L_3.\;
L_1\twoheadrightarrow L_3
\land
L_2\twoheadrightarrow L_3
}
\text{ Church–Rosser}.
$$

So now:

$$
M\twoheadrightarrow L_1\twoheadrightarrow L_3
$$

and therefore, by transitivity of $\twoheadrightarrow$,

$$
M\twoheadrightarrow L_3.
$$

Likewise,

$$
N\twoheadrightarrow L_2\twoheadrightarrow L_3
$$

gives

$$
N\twoheadrightarrow L_3.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\exists L_3.\;
M\twoheadrightarrow L_3
\land
N\twoheadrightarrow L_3
}
$$

which is exactly what Lemma 2.2 wanted.

So schematically, the **transitivity branch of the proof** looks like:

$$
\require{AMScd}
\begin{array}{ccc}
D_1:M=_rL_0 && D_2:L_0=_rN\\[3pt]
\downarrow IH && \downarrow IH\\[3pt]
\begin{matrix}
M\twoheadrightarrow L_1\\
L_0\twoheadrightarrow L_1
\end{matrix}
&&

L_2\twoheadrightarrow L_3
\end{matrix}
&\\[8pt]

M\twoheadrightarrow L_3
\;\land\;
N\twoheadrightarrow L_3
}
&
\end{array}
$$

### 2. The complete induction has several cases

Because $=_r$ is generated from reduction by reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity, the induction branches according to the **last rule of the $=_r$ proof**:

- **Base reduction:** if $M\to_rN$, choose $L=N$. Then $M\twoheadrightarrow N$ and $N\twoheadrightarrow N$.
- **Reflexivity:** $M=_rM$. Choose $L=M$.
- **Symmetry:** if the premise is $N=_rM$, use the IH on that premise; the same common $L$ works.
- **Transitivity:** use IH twice, then Church–Rosser as above.

That is the full proof schema for Lemma 2.2. There isn't one gigantic concrete tree because the lemma is saying **whatever finite $=_r$ proof tree you hand me, I can recursively transform it into evidence that the two endpoints have a common reduct.**

And then the dependency structure for the book's overall consistency theorem is roughly:

$$
\boxed{\text{Theorem 2.1: uniqueness of evaluation}}
$$

depends on

$$
\boxed{\text{Lemma 2.2: }M=_rN\Rightarrow M,N\text{ have a common reduct}}
$$

which depends, in its transitivity case, on

$$
\boxed{\text{Lemma 2.3: Church--Rosser}}
$$

which the book then proves separately.

That's exactly what “proof finished **modulo** another proof” means: **we have built the upper part of the proof tree, but one of its branches is currently a box labeled “Church–Rosser”; now we go prove what's inside that box.**
